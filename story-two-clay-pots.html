The two clay pots

The same beginning does not guarantee the same journey.

“We started in the same place. So why am I not where they are?” If you have ever asked that, this story is for you.

Same clay, same hands

A potter took one mound of clay and split it into two equal pieces. From the same earth, with the same hands, he shaped two pots. Side by side, they looked almost the same.

Not the same fire

The first pot was placed near the hottest part of the kiln. The fire tested every weak point in its walls. The second pot stood farther from the flame and cooled sooner.

After firing, the second pot looked smoother. The first had darkened edges and a small scar where the heat had found a hidden air pocket.

At the market

At the market, people admired the smooth pot first. The scarred pot counted every one of its marks and wondered why the same clay had turned out so differently.

Then came the water

Then the potter filled both with water. The smooth pot began to soften at its base. It had not been in the fire long enough. The scarred pot held firm.

The potter did not call one a success and the other a failure. He looked at each one honestly. One needed another firing. The other could be used, scar and all.

They began with the same clay. They had not lived through the same heat.

What the story shows

Comparison strips out context. We line up the results we can see and ignore timing, money, health, opportunity, support, responsibility and plain luck. The comparison feels exact. It is not fair.

Looking up
Comparing with someone ahead of you can teach you something. It can also turn their milestone into evidence against you.
Looking down
Comparing with someone behind you can bring quick relief. It can also cost you some compassion.
Ask what it is for
Comparison is not always bad. The useful question is what this comparison is doing for you right now.
Equal worth, different paths
People are different and so are their circumstances. Equal worth does not need equal speed, ability or results.

A fair note: the scars are not the point. Hardship does not automatically make anyone stronger, and pain is nothing to wish for. Getting support does not make an achievement less real either.

A fair look at yourself asks four things. What conditions shaped this result? What did I choose? What was outside my control? What can I learn now?

Where it shows up

  • Milestones. “By this age I should have…” ignores caregiving, illness, money, culture and opportunity. A milestone can matter without being a verdict on your whole life.
  • Social media. You see the chosen result. You do not see the private cost, the support behind it or the failed attempts before it.
  • Career. Two people with the same job title may have had very different access to mentors, safety, time and contacts.
  • Self-respect. Your scars do not make you better than anyone. Someone else’s smooth surface does not mean their life was easy.

Five questions worth asking

  1. Whose visible result am I using to judge my private journey?
  2. Which differences in our situations am I ignoring?
  3. What have my hard experiences taught me, and what have they cost me?
  4. What support would help me now, instead of demanding that I get stronger alone?
  5. What measure of progress fits my real values and my real circumstances?

Write your answers in the brain dump

Draw your own kiln map

  1. Name one comparison that keeps getting to you.
  2. List only the facts you truly know about the other person’s journey.
  3. List the conditions that have shaped your own.
  4. Choose one measure of progress that comes from your values.
  5. Write the next step you can take without using someone else’s timeline as your deadline.

You are not behind in someone else’s life. Your job is to understand and shape your own.
